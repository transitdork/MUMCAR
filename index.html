The short answer

Scores are out of 10, weighted for local family driving with home charging, and reflect the best grade priced at or under $60,000 driveaway — so a car can't look better than it really is just because of features that only exist on an expensive top-spec model (tab 6 breaks down exactly what each grade includes). Since fast public charging isn’t a priority, a car’s slow DC speed barely counts — what matters is home charging on a Type 2 wall box.

If the garage gets extended by 40–50cm (new limit: ~4,980–5,030mm), two genuinely strong cars become viable that don't fit today — both would slot into the main list below — the S6 in 5th place (8.1) and the Sealion 7 in 6th (8.0), just behind the four cars scoring 8.2 and above. They're shown here separately rather than mixed into the main ranking, since they depend on building work that hasn't happened yet.

How the scores were calculated

Each car is marked out of 10 in six categories, weighted for a driver who mostly does local trips, values ease and safety, and charges at home. Because you told me fast public charging isn’t a concern, DC charging speed carries almost no weight — on a home Type 2 wall box every car here charges at a similar rate.

Scores reflect the best grade you can buy at or under $60,000 driveaway. This stops a car from looking better than it really is on the strength of features that only appear on an expensive top-spec model your budget wouldn't stretch to. Any grade over $60k is left out of scoring — which is why the Volvo EX40 (from ~$70k) is excluded from the ranking altogether, and why the Kona scores its 360° camera as unavailable (that only comes on its $60,990 Premium, just over the line). See the “Which grade to buy” tab for exactly what each step up adds.

Everyday ease is built up from five sub-scores (shown indented) so you can see exactly why a car scores well or badly on ease — whether it's physical buttons, a simple interface, calm alerts, easy step-in, or easy parking. The overall ease figure is the average of those five. What to watch for, in plain English:

BYD's warranty keeps improving — Evo adds drive-unit cover

Under BYD's original distributor (EVDirect), the old Atto 3's warranty had a confusing patchwork of shorter periods on individual components — lights and suspension covered for only 4 years, infotainment and the charging port for 3 years, the 12V battery for just 1 year. In April 2025, BYD simplified this, removing the shorter carve-outs and backdating the improvement to every Atto 3 since 2022. The Atto 3 Evo carries this forward and adds one more specific: an 8-year/150,000km warranty on the drive unit itself, on top of the standard 6-year/150,000km vehicle warranty and 8-year/160,000km battery warranty.

Sealion 7: 6 years is the shortest vehicle warranty of the two extension cars

BYD gives 6 years/150,000km on the vehicle and 8 years/160,000km on the battery, with no servicing condition. The MG S6 is a year longer on the vehicle (7 years), but MG's battery cover is shorter, so neither is clearly better — BYD's longer battery term matters most for a car kept a long time.

Chery E5's warranty is clean, with no conditions found

Unlike the MG and Hyundai above, the Chery E5's 7-year unlimited-km warranty doesn't appear to come with an on-time-servicing condition attached — it's a straightforward term, backed by an 8-year unlimited-km battery warranty and roughly $1,901 of capped-price servicing spread over 7 years, among the cheapest running costs in this whole comparison. Chery also includes a year of roadside assistance with the option to renew it at each scheduled service, for up to 7 years total.

Hyundai's 7-year warranty has the same catch

As of September 2025, new Hyundais (including Kona Electric) get a 7-year unlimited-km warranty — but only if every scheduled service happens at a Hyundai dealer, on time. Otherwise it's the older 5-year standard. This is a very similar condition to MG's, just starting from a shorter base.

Jaecoo J5 EV's warranty is the strongest on paper here — for a brand with zero track record

An 8-year unlimited-km vehicle warranty, 8-year unlimited-km battery warranty, 8 years of capped-price servicing, and 8 years of roadside assistance is a genuinely outstanding package — on paper, it beats every single car in this comparison, including the established Japanese and Korean brands. The catch isn't in the fine print (no unusual conditions were found), it's that Jaecoo only arrived in Australia in January 2026, so there's no history yet of how BYD-style warranty simplifications or Hyundai-style servicing conditions might later apply, or how smoothly a claim would actually be honoured years from now. A strong promise from a brand with no track record is still worth less certainty than the same promise from Kia or Hyundai.

Leapmotor & Zeekr: straightforward on paper, but young brands

Both list clean, unconditional warranty terms (6yr/150,000km and 5yr unlimited respectively) with no unusual catches found in research. The caveat isn't the paperwork — it's that both are new enough to the Australian market that there's no long track record yet of how smoothly warranty claims actually get handled here in practice.

MG's 10-year warranty has a catch

MG advertises up to 10 years/250,000km, but you only get past the standard 7 years if every service is done at an MG dealer, on schedule. Skip one, or use an independent mechanic even once, and you drop back to 7 years for the rest of ownership. If your Mum wants the longer warranty, she needs to commit to MG's own service centres the whole time.

MG S6 has the same 10-year catch as the S5

The S6 comes with 7 years unlimited km, which can extend to 10 years/250,000km only if every service is done at an MG dealer on schedule. Its battery warranty (7 years) is shorter than the extended vehicle warranty, so ask the dealer to explain how the two terms line up.

Renault is the only capped-kilometre warranty here

Every other car in this comparison has an unlimited-kilometre warranty (or close to it). The Megane E-Tech's is capped at 100,000 km over 5 years — for a low-mileage driver like your Mum (around 7,500 km/year) that's not a practical concern, since she'd take over 13 years to reach it, but it's worth knowing this is the one warranty here with a genuine mileage ceiling.

The Skoda Elroq's HUD isn't standard — it's a $6,000 option pack

This is the one car in this whole comparison where a head-up display genuinely exists under the $60k ceiling, but it's worth being precise about the price: the base Elroq 60 Select ($47,990 before on-roads) doesn't have one. The $6,000 Signature Package is what adds it, bundled together with matrix LED headlights, a 360° camera, premium Canton audio, and other extras — it isn't possible to buy the HUD on its own without the rest of the pack. That pushes the effective price to around $54,000 before on-roads, right at the edge of this comparison's budget once on-road costs are added. Warranty itself is a clean 7-year unlimited-km term with no unusual conditions, unaffected by which options are fitted.

Volvo EX40's servicing is genuinely free, not just capped

Every other car in this comparison offers capped-price servicing — you still pay for each visit, just at a pre-agreed amount. The EX40 is the only one here where Volvo includes 5 years/150,000km of servicing at no cost at all, plus 5 years of roadside assistance, bundled into the purchase price. Its warranty itself is a standard 5-year unlimited-km term with no unusual conditions — the free servicing is the real standout, and it's a genuine point in the EX40's favour against its otherwise steep price.

Zeekr 7X: straightforward terms, but servicing cost isn’t published yet

The vehicle warranty is 5 years unlimited km, and the battery and EV components carry 8 years/160,000km with a 70% state-of-health guarantee. Service is due every 12 months or 20,000km. A 7-year warranty was offered to early orders in 2025, but that offer is over. CarExpert reports the capped-price servicing figures as still “TBC”.

General warranty tips:
• "Whichever comes first" means both the years AND the kilometre limit apply — hitting either one ends that part of the warranty, even if the other hasn't been reached.
• Battery warranties typically guarantee the battery won't degrade below 70–75% of its original capacity within the warranty period — not that it won't degrade at all. Normal, gradual capacity loss is expected and isn't itself a claimable fault.
• Commercial/rideshare use usually shortens warranties across the board (often to 5 years/150,000km) — not relevant for personal use, but worth knowing if the car is ever used commercially.
• Warranties are almost always transferable to a subsequent owner if resold within the period, which helps resale value.
• Always ask the dealer to confirm current terms in writing at the time of purchase — warranty terms do change (as BYD's did in 2025), and dealer paperwork is what actually governs a claim, not a webpage.

Planning the garage wall box
  • A rear port (Sealion, S6) sits at the back of the car. Drive in nose-first and the port ends up by the garage door; reverse in and it is at the back wall. The EX5’s front port is the opposite: back wall if nose-in, door end if reversed in.
  • The side is now confirmed. Driving in nose-first, the car’s right (driver’s) side is the garage wall on your right as you enter, so the Sealion and EX5 want a wall box on the right-hand wall and the S6 on the left-hand wall. Reversing in swaps the sides. Rear port plus nose-in puts the wall box near the door, so the extension adds no cable length for the Sealion or S6.
  • Ask each dealer which cable is supplied and how long it is, then check the distance from the wall box to the port at home ?.
  • Ask about setting a charge limit (80% vs 100%) and a schedule — especially for the S6’s NMC battery.

What the spare length really means. It is the gap between the car’s bumper and the garage wall, with the car pushed fully in. Two things eat into it: walking past the end of the car, and a tailgate that swings back as it opens. As a rule of thumb (mine, not a published figure) about 600–800 mm is comfortable to walk past.
